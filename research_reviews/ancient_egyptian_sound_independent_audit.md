# Ancient Egyptian Sound — independent evidence audit

**Audit date:** 2026-11-13  
**Branch:** `phase2/intelligent-historical-research`  
**Scope:** Independent source/claim assessment of `research_packages/ancient_sound_review_packet.md`.  
**Important:** This is an audit recommendation, not a human approval. No canonical evidence-ledger status or approval field is changed.

## Executive finding

The packet's core direction is supportable if it is framed as **the archaeology of ancient Egyptian music and the modern study of historical sound**, not as evidence for pyramid-construction frequencies, supernatural energy, or a proven ancient science of architectural acoustics.

The three sources are real and relevant, but claim 3 needs a more precise description of the Tell el-Farcha study. The study's numerical reconstructions are models of the likely vibration/acoustic behavior of two archaeological rattles; they are not recordings of ancient performances. The authors also state that ancient Egyptian ceramic vessel rattles are not securely associated with music in surviving iconography or inscriptions.

## Source-by-source assessment

### snd_src_001 — Dendera temple acoustic measurement

**Source:** Sibylle Emerit et al., “Assessing the Acoustics of an Ancient Egyptian Temple,” *2021 Immersive and 3D Audio: from Architecture to Automotive (I3DA)*, DOI [10.1109/I3DA48870.2021.9610973](https://doi.org/10.1109/i3da48870.2021.9610973).

**Recommendation:** APPROVE FOR HUMAN REVIEW — high confidence for the narrow measurement claim.

**What is supported:** The paper reports an acoustic measurement campaign at the main temple of Dendera using a spherical microphone array and post-processing of directional room impulse responses (DRIR). Its abstract identifies the temple as dating to the 1st century BCE–1st century CE and describes the work as a first step toward auralization of a 3D numerical model.

**Critical limitations:** The authors explicitly state that no text has been found showing that an acoustic theory governed the design of Egyptian monuments. They say whether the acoustic properties were intentional or incidental remains debated. The study investigates Dendera, not Old Kingdom pyramid interiors. It cannot support claims about pyramid construction, frequency-powered technology, or deliberate supernatural amplification.

**Suggested ledger wording:** “A 2021 study measured the acoustic properties of the main temple at Dendera using a spherical microphone array and directional room-impulse-response analysis.”

**Human reviewer should inspect:** Abstract and sections II–V, especially the measurement campaign and post-processing. IEEE's public landing page exposes the abstract and section headings, but the full paper was not independently accessible in this audit session. Record exact page numbers from the paper/PDF before ledger approval.

### snd_src_002 — Instrument volume and venue acoustics

**Source:** Heidi Köpp-Junk, “The Sound of Ancient Egypt — Acoustics and Volume Measurements,” *Ars inter Culturas* 9 (2020), pp. 13–33, DOI [10.34858/AIC.9.2020.327](https://doi.org/10.34858/AIC.9.2020.327).

**Recommendation:** APPROVE FOR HUMAN REVIEW — high confidence for the narrow methods/results claim; qualify interpretive statements.

**What is supported:** The article combines textual, iconographic, and archaeological evidence with experimental music-archaeology measurements of replicas of ancient Egyptian instruments and modern equivalents. It discusses documented performance settings including open air, canopies, rooms, tombs, and temples. The paper describes the measurement approach and reports decibel readings for replicas.

**Critical limitations:** The measurements are of replicas/modern equivalents, not surviving ancient performances or original instruments being played in antiquity. The article's discussion of how reverberation may have affected religious experience is interpretive, not proof that Egyptian architects deliberately engineered acoustic effects. The author notes that the sound conditions of a room change with occupancy and furnishings, which makes simple reconstruction of ancient performance conditions uncertain.

**Suggested ledger wording:** “A 2020 music-archaeology study measured the sound levels of replicas of ancient Egyptian instruments and discussed how different performance venues could affect sound.”

**Human reviewer should inspect:** pp. 13–16 for scope and historical evidence; pp. 19–22 for discussion of room acoustics and measurement results; inspect the table and methods before quoting any decibel values.

### snd_src_003 — Tell el-Farcha rattles and numerical reconstruction

**Source:** Katarzyna Tatoń and Ireneusz Czajka, “The Rattles from Tell El-Farcha: Acoustic Research with the Use of Numerical Sound Reconstruction,” *Studies in Ancient Art and Civilization* 25 (2021), pp. 7–29 in the PDF, DOI [10.12797/SAAC.25.2021.25.01](https://doi.org/10.12797/SAAC.25.2021.25.01).

**Recommendation:** APPROVE FOR HUMAN REVIEW — high confidence for the existence and method of the acoustic study; moderate confidence for reconstructed acoustic characteristics because model assumptions are material.

**What is supported:** The authors used short-time Fourier transform analysis and numerical reconstruction to study clay rattles. They first recorded a modern replica of a separate 18th-Dynasty rattle from Sheikh Abd el-Qurnah to test/validate their reconstruction method. They then built geometric models from photographs and drawings of two of the six Tell el-Farcha rattles and ran finite-element/modal analyses because the archaeological objects were not available for recording.

**Critical limitations:** The Tell el-Farcha objects themselves were not recorded. The models depend on assumptions about the rattles' internal geometry, wall thickness, and material properties. The authors state that the model's frequency lines do not indicate sound intensity, and that a coupled analysis needed to determine acoustic pressure amplitudes was not performed. The article also says ancient Egyptian ceramic vessel rattles are not securely associated with music in surviving iconography or inscriptions. Do not describe these as proof of the exact sounds ancient Egyptians heard, or confidently call the objects musical instruments used in performance.

**Suggested ledger wording:** “A 2021 study analyzed a modern replica of an Egyptian rattle and used numerical models based on photographs and drawings to investigate the possible vibration frequencies of two clay rattles from Tell el-Farcha.”

**Human reviewer should inspect:** pp. 10–15 for the replica recording, reconstruction assumptions, and model limitations; pp. 14–18 for Tell el-Farcha modeling and conclusions.

## Editorial guardrails

- Keep Dendera's Ptolemaic/Roman-era context (1st century BCE–1st century CE) separate from Old Kingdom pyramid construction.
- No evidence in these sources shows sound frequencies were used to build the pyramids.
- Do not say temple acoustics were proven to be intentionally engineered. The Dendera authors explicitly leave intention unresolved.
- Distinguish physical measurements of modern replicas, numerical modeling, and historical inference.
- Do not use “the exact sound of ancient Egypt” as a factual promise. A reconstruction can be illustrative, but its assumptions must be disclosed.
- Avoid quantum healing, frequency medicine, supernatural-energy, and pyramid-resonance claims.

## Recommended factual episode framing

**Title:** “What Did Ancient Egypt Sound Like? The Science of Reconstructing the Past”

**Hook:** “We cannot press play on an ancient Egyptian temple. But researchers can measure replica instruments, study the acoustics of surviving spaces, and model how ancient objects may have vibrated.”

**Narrative boundary:** Make clear that modern acoustic measurements tell us about surviving spaces and replicas today. They do not reproduce an ancient ceremony with certainty.
